How much protein do you really need?

The guidelines describe minimums, the internet describes maximums, and most people sit somewhere confused in between. Here is what the evidence actually supports, and how to find your own number.

What the guidelines actually say

The Institute of Medicine's recommended dietary allowance is 0.8 g of protein per kilogram of body weight per day. That figure is widely quoted and widely misunderstood: it is the amount that prevents deficiency in nearly all healthy adults, not the amount that is optimal for a given person's activity, age or goal. The WHO's safe level of 0.83 g/kg says the same thing in slightly different words.

For older adults, the PROT-AGE and ESPEN groups raise the floor to 1.0–1.2 g/kg, because muscle responds less efficiently to protein with age. For people who train, the ISSN and ACSM place the range at 1.2–2.0 g/kg…

Why the highest applicable band wins

Each guideline describes a minimum for a population. If you belong to more than one of those populations, the most demanding minimum is the one that applies to you. A 68-year-old who lifts weights three times a week is both an older adult and an exerciser, so the training band governs…

Guidelines describe minimums. Take the most demanding one that applies to you, and stop at 2.0 g/kg.

Age, training and plant-based diets

Plant proteins are digested a little less completely than animal proteins, which is why a vegetarian or vegan target is nudged upward. That adjustment is an extrapolation from digestibility data rather than a guideline figure, and any honest tool should say so…

When a calculator should refuse to answer

In chronic kidney disease, protein guidance runs in the opposite direction: national kidney guidelines recommend a reduced, supervised intake. A general-population number is not merely unhelpful there, it could cause harm. That is why our tool gives no figure at all when you say you have kidney disease, and sends you to your renal team instead.
